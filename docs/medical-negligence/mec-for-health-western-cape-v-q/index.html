MEC for Health, Western Cape v Q

(928/2017) [2018] ZASCA 132
28 September 2018

Supreme Court of Appeal — Medical Negligence, Expert Evidence, Causation and the Onus of Proof.

How to use this case: Read the judgment continuously. Selected legal principles appear in purple. Click a purple principle to examine its meaning, application to the facts, possible submission, opposing submission and advocacy question.

Judgment

[1] This is an appeal against a judgment of the Western Cape Division of the High Court, Cape Town. The High Court found the appellant, the Member of the Executive Council for Health, Western Cape, liable for damages suffered by the respondent's minor daughter, I.Q., as a result of cerebral palsy. The appeal concerns the correctness of that finding.

[2] I.Q. was born at Paarl Hospital on 7 December 2011. She suffers from spastic quadriplegic cerebral palsy. Her mother instituted action against the appellant in 2013. The High Court found that the medical staff had been negligent and awarded damages on the basis that the appellant was liable for 100 per cent of the respondent's proven damages.

[3] The appellant appeals against that judgment. The central question is whether the respondent discharged the onus of proving that the cerebral palsy was caused by negligence on the part of the medical staff.

[4] The respondent's antenatal history included a persistent urinary tract infection. Various antenatal investigations were conducted during the pregnancy.

[5] On 30 November 2011 a non-stress test was conducted. The evidence showed that the test was incorrectly assessed as reactive. According to the respondent's expert evidence, the patient should not have been sent home.

[6] On 7 December 2011 the respondent returned to hospital. A further non-stress test was non-reactive and demonstrated poor baseline variability. Labour was induced.

[7] I.Q. was born at approximately 23h15. She was microcephalic and had a cephalohaematoma. There was evidence of intracerebral bleeding. Her cord blood pH was 7.22 and her Apgar scores were 4, 7 and 9.

[8] The child subsequently developed hypoxic ischaemic encephalopathy, seizures and hydrocephalus. Radiological investigations were conducted after birth.

[9] The respondent pleaded various grounds of negligence, including failures to investigate danger signs, failure to intervene timeously by Caesarean section and failure properly to monitor the labour and fetal heart rate.

[10] An alternative allegation concerned the failure to cool the baby after birth because of an alleged lack of available beds.

[11] The issues before the High Court included the damage-causing occurrence, the timing of that occurrence, whether the medical staff had been negligent and whether the negligence was causally linked to the child's cerebral palsy.

[12] The respondent called Professor Andronikou, a radiologist, Dr Mtsi, an obstetrician and gynaecologist, and Dr Reid, a clinical neurologist as expert witnesses.

[13] The appellant called Professor van Toorn, a paediatric neurologist, and Professor Odendaal, a gynaecologist and obstetrician.

[14] The experts prepared a joint minute. There was agreement that the respondent should not have been sent home after the non-reactive NST on 30 November and that a Caesarean section should have been performed. There was also agreement that continuous CTG monitoring should have been conducted during delivery. The quality of the recordal was poor.

[15] The experts disagreed about the due date, an alleged NST on 23 November, and whether intervention on 7 December would have prevented the child's injuries.

[16] The respondent testified and relied principally upon the evidence of Dr Mtsi and Dr Reid.

[17] Dr Mtsi considered that the urinary tract infection required further investigation and hospital admission.

[18] Professor Odendaal considered that a connection between the urinary tract infection and cerebral palsy was improbable. She relied upon medical literature which did not establish such a link.

[19] The evidence regarding the 30 November NST was also disputed. Dr Mtsi considered that a Caesarean section could have saved the baby. Professor Odendaal considered that the damage had probably already occurred by then.

[20] On 7 December both obstetric experts agreed that a Caesarean section should have occurred.

[21] Professor van Toorn explained that microcephaly develops over a period of weeks. He considered the CTG evidence, the clinical findings and the criteria for hypoxic ischaemic encephalopathy.

[22] Professor van Toorn did not identify a sentinel event which explained the child's condition. The Apgar scores also improved after birth.

[23] The High Court found that the medical staff had breached their duty of care. It did not, however, make a clear finding as to precisely when the damage had occurred.

[24] The High Court did not expressly explain why it preferred the evidence of Dr Mtsi over that of the appellant's experts.

[25] The High Court appeared to infer that the child had suffered a birth injury because Professor van Toorn could not state with absolute certainty that the injury had occurred before labour, and because contemporaneous records referred to birth asphyxia.

[26] The appellant submitted that there was no adequate causal link between any negligent conduct and the cerebral palsy.

[27] The respondent relied upon the evidence suggesting that the failure to intervene caused or contributed to the child's injury.

[28] The person who asserts that a damage-causing event occurred bears the onus of proving that event.

[29] The duty of medical staff is not to exercise the highest possible degree of skill but to exercise the general level of skill and diligence possessed and exercised by the ordinary skilled practitioner in the relevant profession.

[30] The respondent therefore had to establish, by credible and persuasive evidence, that the doctors and nurses failed to meet the applicable standard of care.

[31] Expert evidence must have a logical basis and must be supported by proper reasons.

[32] The probative value of an expert opinion depends significantly upon the reasons furnished for the opinion. An expert must demonstrate a proper basis for the conclusions reached.

[33] An expert conclusion cannot carry sufficient weight where the factual or scientific foundation for that conclusion is inadequate.

[34] Dr Mtsi's evidence contained weaknesses. She did not identify a specific risk arising from the urinary tract infection and there was no study establishing a connection between the infection and cerebral palsy.

[35] There were also factual difficulties in Dr Mtsi's interpretation of the records, including difficulties arising from her interpretation of Afrikaans entries.

[36] Her evidence did not establish a sufficient basis for an early Caesarean section on the facts relied upon.

[37] The references in the records to birth asphyxia did not necessarily establish when the injury had occurred.

[38] Dr Mtsi accepted that the expression “birth asphyxia” was non-descriptive. Professor van Toorn's opinion was based upon the CTG evidence and information available after the birth.

[39] Dr Reid preferred an intrapartum explanation, but his field was adult neurology. The allegation concerning a ventouse delivery was also conjectural and inconsistent with the documentary record.

[40] Expert evidence founded on established facts and supported by logical reasoning is more persuasive than an opinion resting on speculation.

[41] Professor Odendaal and Professor van Toorn's opinions were founded on clearly established facts and were logical and well reasoned.

[42] The Court was required to assess the expert opinions according to their logical foundation, the facts upon which they rested and the reasons advanced for the conclusions.

[43] Although a reasonable practitioner would not have sent the patient home on 30 November, the evidence indicated that the damage had probably already occurred by that time.

[44] Proof of negligent conduct does not by itself establish that the negligent conduct caused the plaintiff's damage.

[45] The evidence did not establish a causal link between the failure to intervene and the damage. The same applied to the alleged negligence after 30 November.

[46] The cause and timing of the child's injury remained unidentified.

[47] It is impermissible to reason backwards from the existence of harm to the conclusion that the defendant caused that harm negligently.

[48] Harm alone is not proof that medical staff caused the harm, caused it negligently, or that the harm resulted from a particular negligent act or omission.

[49] The fact that the child suffered severe neurological injury could not, without more, establish the causal connection required in delict.

[50] The approach of reasoning backwards from effect to cause was impermissible. The Court referred to the principle stated in Goliath v MEC for Health, Eastern Cape, that it is not permissible to reason backwards from the consequence to the cause.

[51] Where the plaintiff fails to establish the damage-causing event and the causal link on a balance of probabilities, the claim cannot succeed merely because negligence occurred.

Order

1. The appeal is upheld with costs, including the costs of two counsel.

2. The order of the High Court is set aside and replaced with:

“The plaintiff's claim is dismissed with costs, including the costs of two counsel.”

Advocacy Practice

The central advocacy question in this case is not simply whether the medical staff acted negligently. It is whether the evidence establishes that the negligent conduct caused the child's damage.

Question: If negligence is admitted or established, what additional evidence must the plaintiff produce to succeed?
Possible submission for the plaintiff: The failure to investigate the abnormal NST, the failure to intervene timeously and the failure properly to monitor the fetal heart rate constituted breaches of the required standard of care. The clinical and expert evidence, considered cumulatively, supports the inference that these failures contributed materially to the child's neurological injury.
Possible submission for the MEC: Even if the medical staff were negligent, negligence alone does not establish causation. The plaintiff must prove when the injury occurred and that the negligent conduct probably caused or materially contributed to it. The expert evidence supporting that proposition was speculative and insufficiently reasoned, whereas the appellant's experts provided logical explanations founded on established facts.
