Case 02

Road Accident Fund v Russell

Case No. 656/98 · Supreme Court of Appeal · 24 November 2000

Case Overview

This case concerns legal causation and the question whether the subsequent suicide of a person whose mental functioning had been impaired by injuries sustained in a motor vehicle accident constitutes a novus actus interveniens that breaks the causal chain.

01 · The Facts

The deceased sustained serious brain injuries in a motor vehicle accident. Following the accident, his mental functioning and judgment were affected and he developed depression.

He subsequently committed suicide. The question arose whether his death could still legally be attributed to the injuries caused by the accident.

02 · The Legal Issue

The central question was whether the suicide constituted a novus actus interveniens — a new intervening act which interrupted the chain of causation between the motor vehicle accident and the deceased's death.

03 · Causation

The case demonstrates that establishing factual causation is only part of the enquiry. The Court also had to determine whether the connection between the accident, the resulting brain injury, the impaired mental state and the eventual death was sufficiently close for legal causation to exist.

04 · Novus Actus Interveniens

A subsequent event does not automatically break the chain of causation. The nature of the intervening conduct and the circumstances in which it occurred must be considered.

Here, the deceased's impaired mental condition was central to the Court's reasoning.

05 · The Court's Reasoning

The Court considered the effect of the brain injury on the deceased's ability to make a rational and balanced decision. His depression and impaired judgment were consequences of the injuries sustained in the accident.

The Court therefore considered the suicide in the context of the deceased's impaired mental condition rather than treating it as an entirely independent event disconnected from the original injuries.

The Central Question

Did the deceased's suicide constitute a novus actus interveniens which broke the chain of legal causation between the motor vehicle accident and his death?

Principle from the Case

The Court held that the suicide did not constitute a novus actus interveniens in the circumstances of the case. The deceased's brain injury had materially affected his mental functioning and judgment, and that impairment was relevant to the causal connection between the accident and his eventual death.

The case therefore illustrates an important distinction in RAF causation: a later act may appear to be an independent event, but it does not necessarily break the causal chain where the original injury materially contributes to the condition that produces the later event.

06 · Practitioner Lesson

When analysing causation, do not stop at identifying a later event. Ask whether the original accident or injury materially contributed to the circumstances in which that event occurred.

07 · Key Concept

Novus actus interveniens

A new intervening act which is sufficiently independent of the original wrongful conduct may break the chain of causation. Russell demonstrates that this depends on the particular facts and the relationship between the original injury and the later event.

08 · Why It Matters

The case is useful when a RAF claim involves complications, subsequent conduct or events occurring after the original accident. It shows why factual causation and legal causation must be analysed separately.
